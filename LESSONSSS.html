Introduction to Application of
Geometric Sequences and Series

Lesson 01  •  Building Strong Foundations

1. What is a Sequence?

A sequence is an ordered list of numbers that follow a particular pattern or rule. Each number in the sequence is called a term. Sequences can be finite (having a limited number of terms) or infinite (continuing forever).

There are two main types of sequences you will meet in mathematics:

  • Arithmetic Sequence – each term is obtained by adding a constant number (common difference).
  • Geometric Sequence – each term is obtained by multiplying by a constant number (common ratio).

2. What is a Geometric Sequence?

A geometric sequence (also called a geometric progression) is a sequence in which each term after the first is found by multiplying the previous term by a fixed, non-zero number called the common ratio (denoted by r).

The general form of a geometric sequence is:

a,   ar,   ar2,   ar3,   ar4,   ar5,   …

Where:

  • a or a1 = the first term
  • r = the common ratio
  • an = the nth term
  • n = the position of the term in the sequence

How to find the Common Ratio

The common ratio is found by dividing any term by the term that comes before it:

r = a2 ÷ a1 = a3 ÷ a2 = a4 ÷ a3 = …

Example 1 – Identifying a Geometric Sequence

Is the sequence 5, 10, 20, 40, 80, … geometric?

Check the ratio between consecutive terms:
10 ÷ 5 = 2
20 ÷ 10 = 2
40 ÷ 20 = 2
80 ÷ 40 = 2

Since the ratio is constant (r = 2), the sequence is geometric.

Example 2 – Finding the Common Ratio

Find the common ratio of the sequence: 81, 27, 9, 3, 1, …

r = 27 ÷ 81 = 1/3
Check: 9 ÷ 27 = 1/3,   3 ÷ 9 = 1/3,   1 ÷ 3 = 1/3

The common ratio is r = 1/3 (or approximately 0.333).

3. The nth Term Formula

Instead of writing out every term, we can find any term directly using the general formula:

an = a1 × rn−1

This formula is one of the most important tools in geometric sequences.

Example 3 – Finding a Specific Term

Find the 8th term of the geometric sequence: 3, 6, 12, 24, …

Step 1: Identify a1 and r
a1 = 3
r = 6 ÷ 3 = 2
Step 2: Apply the formula
a8 = 3 × 28−1 = 3 × 27 = 3 × 128 = 384

Example 4 – Finding a Term when r is a Fraction

Find the 6th term of the sequence: 64, 32, 16, 8, …

a1 = 64,   r = 32 ÷ 64 = 1/2
a6 = 64 × (1/2)5 = 64 × 1/32 = 2

4. Geometric Series

When we add the terms of a geometric sequence, we obtain a geometric series.

Sn = a1 + a1r + a1r2 + a1r3 + … + a1rn−1

Sum of the First n Terms

There are two important formulas depending on the value of r:

Sn = a1 (rn − 1) ÷ (r − 1)    when r ≠ 1
Sn = n × a1    when r = 1
Tip: An alternative form of the sum formula is
Sn = a1 (1 − rn) ÷ (1 − r)   (useful when |r| < 1)

Example 5 – Sum of a Finite Geometric Series

Find the sum of the first 6 terms of the sequence: 2, 6, 18, 54, …

a1 = 2,   r = 3,   n = 6
S6 = 2 (36 − 1) ÷ (3 − 1)
S6 = 2 (729 − 1) ÷ 2
S6 = 2 × 728 ÷ 2 = 728

Example 6 – Sum when r is Negative

Find the sum of the first 5 terms of: 4, −8, 16, −32, …

a1 = 4,   r = −2,   n = 5
S5 = 4 [(−2)5 − 1] ÷ (−2 − 1)
S5 = 4 (−32 − 1) ÷ (−3)
S5 = 4 (−33) ÷ (−3) = 4 × 11 = 44

5. Infinite Geometric Series

An infinite geometric series continues forever. Whether it has a finite sum depends on the common ratio.

If |r| < 1, the infinite geometric series converges and its sum is finite.
If |r| ≥ 1, the series diverges (the sum grows without limit).
S∞ = a1 ÷ (1 − r)    when |r| < 1

Example 7 – Infinite Series

Find the sum of the infinite series: 9 + 3 + 1 + 1/3 + 1/9 + …

a1 = 9,   r = 3 ÷ 9 = 1/3
Since |1/3| < 1, the series converges.
S∞ = 9 ÷ (1 − 1/3) = 9 ÷ (2/3) = 9 × 3/2 = 13.5
Important: Always check the value of |r| before using the infinite sum formula. If |r| ≥ 1, the formula cannot be applied.

6. Real-Life Applications of Geometric Sequences & Series

Geometric sequences and series are not just abstract mathematics — they appear in many practical situations.

6.1 Compound Interest

When money is invested at compound interest, the amount grows geometrically.

A = P (1 + r)n

where P = principal, r = interest rate per period, n = number of periods.

Example 8

You invest $2 000 at 5% compound interest per year. How much will you have after 4 years?

A = 2000 (1 + 0.05)4 = 2000 (1.05)4 = 2000 × 1.21550625 ≈ $2 431.01

6.2 Population Growth

Populations of bacteria, animals or people often increase by a roughly constant percentage each year, forming a geometric sequence.

6.3 Depreciation of Assets

Cars, machinery and electronic devices lose value over time. If the value decreases by a fixed percentage each year, the values form a geometric sequence (with 0 < r < 1).

6.4 Radioactive Decay

The amount of a radioactive substance remaining after successive half-lives follows a geometric sequence with r = 1/2.

6.5 Other Applications

  • Spread of viruses or information on social media
  • Fractals and self-similar patterns in nature (trees, coastlines, snowflakes)
  • Loan repayments and annuities
  • Sound intensity and musical scales

7. Summary of Key Formulas

nth term:   an = a1 × rn−1
Sum of first n terms:   Sn = a1 (rn − 1) ÷ (r − 1)   (r ≠ 1)
Infinite sum:   S∞ = a1 ÷ (1 − r)   (|r| < 1)
Common ratio:   r = an+1 ÷ an
